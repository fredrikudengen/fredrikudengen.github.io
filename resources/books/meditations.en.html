This was a very thought-provoking book, and I liked it. The prose and format invite you to think and reflect for yourself: short paragraphs or sentences with metaphorical images. I think it is a book you can read several times, and its content will shift with your perspective. Marcus was obviously a reflective and introspective person, and I’m glad I got an insight into his thoughts through this book. I already had an impression of Stoicism before reading it, but it was based on what I had seen and read online. The content of the book surprised me. I wouldn’t call myself a Stoic, but I have gained more respect for Stoicism after this book, at least. All in all, a very good book.

Marcus begins by listing all the virtues he has gotten from others he knows, and thanks them for it. It reminds me of a conversation a friend, Ferdinand, and I had about being conscious of the influences and biases you pick up by living and interacting with others. If you are aware of them, you can begin to deconstruct them and ask yourself why and how you got them, and whether they are something you want to have or be. I think the deconstruction should come before the gratitude: you should ask what a new habit or way of thinking leads to, who it benefits, and what society would look like if everyone did the same (a bit Kantian, that). You can carry on like this indefinitely, and I don’t think you can ever fully understand yourself, but I think it is healthy to reflect anyway.

In this way you can, in a sense, build your own character. But “own” is a difficult word. I don’t believe in free will, and then the will to break out of what you have become aware of is also a product of nature and nurture. Either the sum of what has shaped you allows you to break out, or you end up on another path. Whether you are able to, you don’t know until you try.

“(…) the certainty to ignore the dice of fortune, and have no other perspective, even for a moment, than that of reason alone; to be always the same man unchanged in sudden pain, in the loss of a child, in lingering sickness;” p. 4, book 1.

The quote touches on a controversial topic in Stoicism: how should one handle emotions? What I find interesting is that it says “unchanged” and not “unmoved”. The Stoics themselves distinguish between first reactions and true emotions (_pathē_). Everyone has first reactions, but grief over a child is a _pathos_, because it judges the loss to be an evil. For the Stoics there is only one true evil, moral vice, while death and illness are “indifferent things”. You may cry, as long as you do not judge the loss to be an inherent evil.

Here I disagree, and preventability is the reason. Marcus lived during the Antonine Plague and lost most of his children, so for him child death was an everyday occurrence and there was little to be done about it. The acceptance in the quote suited his world. But today much of what we call “the order of nature” can be prevented, and the causality behind child death is easier to trace. For me, the negative lies in the fact that something could have been prevented, but then it is not a fixed formula either: it changes with what we are able to do something about.

I would never use the word “evil” myself. I don’t believe anyone is evil or good in themselves. Those are concepts we use about behavior, and behavior is the sum of nature and nurture. But I think we should still use words like responsibility, because they are instruments for inhibiting behavior we want less of. What society needs is not to figure out who “deserves” the blame, but to figure out what works.

Consider a child with an illness where certain substances in food, toys or paint are known to make it worse. Then I think the responsibility lies with the parents for what happens at home, with the school for what is served there, with the politicians for what is permitted, and with the advertising industry for the conditions it creates for people’s relationship to food. It is collective and distributed. There is also a real danger of abdication of responsibility with my position: if everyone is responsible, no one is responsible alone.

This is connected to the fact that I don’t believe in free will. I think humans are the sum of nature and nurture, and that we could not have willed otherwise. Yet we all experience making choices, and no argument can take that experience away from us. I see it as two planes: on the absolute plane, I think we are determined; on the other, we can be free. The latter is the plane where concepts like responsibility, coercion and freedom have a function, and where the distinction between someone who wants to and someone who doesn’t can make a difference. That may sound like a contradiction, but I see it more as two different answers to two different questions. Accepting that I am not really free has, paradoxically, made me feel freer.

“The present moment is equal for all; so what is passing is equal also; the loss therefore turns out to be the merest fragment of time. No one can lose either the past or the future - how could anyone be deprived of what he does not possess? (…) both the longest-lived and the earliest to die suffer the same loss. It is only the present moment of which either stands to be deprived: and if indeed this is all he has, he cannot lose what he does not have.” p. 14, book 2.

Marcus argues here that it is fundamentally just as sad to die young as to die old. The only thing you lose is the present, and it is the same for everyone. You don’t have the past or the future, so you cannot lose them either. There is probably some use in accepting death the way Marcus argues for. You can’t do anything about the fact that someone has passed away. But on the other hand it is a somewhat weak argument, and the most obvious counterargument is of course that you lose potential. You lose plans, dreams and ideas. You lose the future.

It is interesting to interpret these quotes through a man who has experienced a lot of death around him. Instead of thinking of him as a man explaining how the world and life are, one can think of him as a man trying to figure out how to live. He is trying to argue for a perspective that will make it easier for him to cope with grief. These were, after all, private notes, and were not meant to have an audience. Perhaps Marcus sat down after he had lost someone and thought about exactly what he had written here. Perhaps it made him feel a little better.

“All is as thinking makes it so.” p. 14, book 2.

Objects in the world have no inherent value; they are neither good nor bad. For a farmer rain can be good, for others bad. Your perspective determines how the world is experienced. I agree that value is a human concept: we project it onto things based on certain factors, and we need values for a society to function, much as we need responsibility. They are instruments on the human plane. At the same time, I think I am reading Marcus as more relativistic here than he is, since the Stoics make an exception for virtue, which they believe has real value.

This means I cannot say that my view of life is truer than others’. In the end it is a matter of different preferences. But I think my preferences follow better from the basic assumptions that politicians and Marcus would probably also largely share (cooperation, equality, the possibility of self-realization). When I criticize them, I am really saying: your actions do not follow from the premises we both agree on, and I think my ideas do. That is the kind of moral discussion I believe in, one that starts from assumptions and not from absolute truths.

This is also where preventability comes in. Avoidable child death is not absolutely worse than child death caused by chance. But I hold the assumption that one should minimize death within reasonable expectations, and from that assumption the preventable death is worse.

Pain is an interesting borderline case. That it hurts to burn yourself can be explained biologically and evolutionarily, and I cannot think the qualia away. But _judging_ the pain as something evil is a projection, and it can change. Many people find spiders and snakes frightening for evolutionary reasons, and yet the fear can be overcome. In the same way, I think one can learn to accept, and perhaps even like, things that involve pain, without judging them as an evil. That is not so far from the Stoics’ own distinction between the first impression, which is involuntary, and judging something as inherently bad.

“Men seek retreats for themselves - in the country, by the sea, in the hills - and you yourself are particularly prone to this yearning. But all this is quite unphilosophic, when it is open to you, at any time you want, to retreat into yourself. No retreat offers someone more quiet and relaxation than that into his own mind, especially if he can dip into thoughts there which put him at immediate and complete ease: and by ease I simply mean a well-ordered life. So constantly give yourself this retreat, and renew yourself. The doctrines you will visit there should be few and fundamental, sufficient at one meeting to wash away all your pain and send you back free of resentment at what you must rejoin.” p. 21, book 4

Why take a physical vacation when you can just as well take a mental one whenever you want? I think much of the stress we live with is connected to our surroundings. That is probably why so many people go on vacation, preferably to warmer climates: you change your mental surroundings indirectly by changing the physical ones. I have nothing against that, and I enjoy mountain hikes myself, which I use as a kind of meditation. My problem is the idea that the change of place itself is the recovery. Behind “I’m looking forward to going on vacation so I can relax” lies an assumption that you can’t relax where you are now, and the vacation becomes an excuse to finally allow yourself to. I find it sad that so many “live for vacation”: they work and struggle, get rescued by a vacation where they can recharge their batteries, and then struggle on until the next break.

At the same time, this is not just an individual problem. That people need vacations to switch off is as much about work culture as about how good we are at relaxing, and “retreating into yourself” can turn a structural problem into a private one. That is a well-known criticism of Stoicism as self-help, and it is a fair one. The main focus should be on structural change. But while working toward it, you can help yourself as much as you can. Meditating doesn’t solve a burned-out society, but it can help with everyday stress and with handling it individually. It is not a replacement, just a concrete method.

I also think the “mental vacation” is worth practicing precisely because it doesn’t require money, much time or a big opportunity. A physical vacation is a reasonable aid as long as you can afford it, but an aid can easily become an emergency solution you can’t get away from.

“Everything in any way beautiful has its beauty of itself, inherent and self-sufficient: praise is no part of it. At any rate, praise does not make anything better or worse. This applies even to the popular conception of beauty, as in material things or works of art. So does the truly beautiful need anything beyond itself? No more than law, no more than truth, no more than kindness or integrity. Which of these things derives its beauty from praise, or withers under criticism? Does an emerald lose its quality if it is not praised? And what of gold, ivory, purple, a lyre, a dagger, a flower, a bush?” p. 27, book 4

Most people know that, to a certain extent, you shouldn’t let criticism stop you, but what about a lack of praise? Marcus makes a point here: you should not depend on praise to feel your own worth. Easier said than done, obviously, but it is probably a very good trait to have.

The quote itself is about beauty, and there I disagree: I don’t think things have any inherent value. It is the observer who gives a work its value, either personally (a film I like a lot) or collectively (books we consider classics). But stretching the quote to apply to people is more interesting. You are not the praise or the criticism you receive. But what should you use them for? When should you not let criticism stop you, and when is someone with experience trying to guide you? I don’t have an answer to that.

The problem is that it is easy to confuse rejecting praise entirely, or finding all praise superficial, with not being dependent on it. If you reject praise entirely, you can easily become insecure, because you have no one to confirm what you are unsure about. I think many modern Stoics, that is, young (men) who want to become more independent, miss the mark a bit here, and that it will most likely backfire later in life, unfortunately.

I don’t know where the line between needing and being dependent goes, and I don’t know whether there is a formal difference. I think you have to feel it for yourself. If you post something on social media because it has been a while since last time, it leans more toward dependence than if you send some pictures to a friend because you want to show them something. I think humans are a sharing creature. We like to show others what we have done and what we are proud of, and that is not necessarily unhealthy. I read a quote online that I always keep in the back of my mind: “You must learn to enjoy life, without needing an audience to see that you are enjoying life”.

“I have often wondered how it is that everyone loves himself more than anyone else, but rates his own judgement of himself below that of others. Anyway, if a god or some wise tutor appeared at his side and told him to entertain no internal thought or intention which he won’t immediately broadcast outside, he would not tolerate this regime for a single day. So it is that we have more respect for what our neighbours will think of us than we have for ourselves.” p. 116, book 12

Marcus argues that we respect what our neighbors think of us more than we respect ourselves. I think it is more accurate to say that we fear it. You can be very hard on yourself, but if others are hard on you, it feels almost totally destructive. Naturalistically it makes sense: being alone has historically been incredibly dangerous, so belonging to the pack was advantageous, and being judged and, in the worst case, cast out would have been a death sentence. It is about survival.

I also think part of the explanation lies in uncertainty. In our own mind we can be unsure about what others think of us, and that uncertainty allows us to be both kind and harsh to ourselves. A healthy mind lets itself be kind sometimes. When others deliver their verdict, the uncertainty disappears, and perhaps it is most dangerous because it can confirm what we already fear about ourselves.

Marcus’s distinction between one’s own judgment and that of others is not so clear in my opinion. My own judgment is shaped by others, but it is my unique synthesis of them, just as theirs is their own. I don’t have control over that. Still, I think one should trust oneself, because if you never do, you are just the sum of others’ opinions, and society loses its variation. Every individual is an experiment, and it is worth trying out. Trusting yourself doesn’t mean believing you are right, but treating your own judgment as a hypothesis, not as an answer key.
