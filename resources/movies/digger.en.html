Layer 1: The Farce

Digger, an oil baron of the good old American type we all love, and his gang of rich, white, older men in positions of power against younger, nerdy scientists in a fight over the world. Nothing new here, nothing deeper. Theatrically exaggerated characters in Kafkaesque situations they are themselves complicit in. It’s entertaining, funny, sad (the characters unfortunately reflect quite a few real people) and incredibly beautiful to look at; the set design is absolutely fantastic. The acting style is violent and loud, and if you can get on board with it, it works well.

“Oscar bait” is what I’ve seen people call Cruise’s performance. I think that’s a somewhat demeaning term to use about art, a bit hipster-ish and anti-intellectual, but that’s their business. To me this is Cruise at his best, in the same vein as Adam Sandler in Uncut Gems: he plays the role exactly as it needs to be played. And the film was, for me, even in all its predictability, incredibly gripping. Take the meeting with the European leaders:

Dr. Ganesh has made it clear that he will tell the truth, so when he walks to the podium surrounded by a roaring crowd of EU politicians, we all know he’s going to pour gasoline straight onto the fire: “Digger would fry your children if it meant he could make money off of them.” This is the sea of flames that meets Digger, and we wait tensely to see how he will be executed (figuratively). The bass drowns out the shouts from the crowd as he walks toward the podium. What does he choose to say? Perhaps surprisingly: the truth. Everything Ganesh said is true. Digger stands at the edge of the cliff and jumps off. But that’s not all he does. He delivers a long speech as he walks across the tables, shoving people aside, saying that it is they who pay for his oil, they who have invested in the company and profit from it doing well, they who scroll TikTok and Instagram and use overnight shipping and all the other goods the industrialized world gives them. While this goes on, the shouts from the hall become more and more chant-like and the scene becomes almost musical. It ends with him leaving the room while the politicians fight among themselves. Perhaps he achieved what he wanted.

Digger points the finger at the top leaders: they have themselves profited from what they are now standing there protesting. It’s true, one has to admit, but, if anything, a bit cheap coming from Digger himself. He has a vested interest in spreading the blame onto everyone else. Nothing stopped him from following his scientists’ advice or cutting his profits to emit less. He takes an absolute position: since you need and use my oil, you are complicit in everything I’ve done, which suits him very well. Directed at the EU’s top politicians, people who actually have real power and the ability to influence climate policy, I think he may have a point.

But if this were all the film was, it wouldn’t have been an Iñárritu film.

Layer 2: The Theater

It turns out the whole story is a play. Cruise plays the actor Curtis, who plays Digger. This gives Iñárritu much more room to explore and experiment. But when a film breaks the fourth wall in this way, the viewer is pulled right out of the story, and this is where many films struggle: if they can’t reel you back in, they lose everything. For me, it managed. There were now two layers to keep track of: the entertaining theater and the presentation of it. I sat there constantly thinking about Iñárritu’s angle. What is he trying to say with this film? Is he trying to make a satire of the satire?

Iñárritu gives us small drips (pun intended) that there is a real world outside the play. Throughout the film the roof leaks, there are buckets on the floor and water damage everywhere. Between the theater segments we learn that Curtis’s daughter is missing. A hurricane is ravaging the east coast of the American continent, and Jamaica is without power. With this in the back of his mind, Curtis keeps playing one of the contributing causes of such extreme weather. For him, the play gets a real face. It becomes close and personal.

Then Curtis interrupts a scene because he thinks the play is becoming too unrealistic: the president and vice president would never be on the same plane. “Mr. Rockwell wasn’t all just ‘Bang! Bang! Bang!’ and cartoony! I have studied the footage, he was composed.” The play has become a farce, he says, a comedy to entertain and laugh at. The director, Hannah, objects that it’s satire, meant to mock Curtis’s generation, the generation that let this happen and fucked things up for those who came after. Curtis rolls his eyes and mocks her a little when she accuses his generation. Curtis wanted to _play_ Digger, to tell the story more as it was. Hannah wanted to make a point of it.

Then Kenny cuts in, the nephew of the real Rockwell and the one who has financed the play. It doesn’t matter whether it’s farce, satire, comedy or tragedy, he says. All they are trying to do is give the audience, who pay to see the play, some relief from their polluted world and a place to vent their minds against his uncle. That is really the best he and these artists can do now that the world is ruined: tell the story anew to help society vent its rage and brand the perpetrators as villains.

It is in this triangle drama that I think the heart of the film lies. Each of the three has a view of what art is, which is connected to what art does for them. Curtis wants to be taken seriously as an actor and handles it poorly when everyone just laughs. He wants the play to tell what happened, without a built-in moral or point. Hannah, on the other hand, has put a lot of work into the play and has to believe it does something: we mock what we dislike from the past so that we can stop it from happening in the present. Art has (for her), if not a purpose, then at least a hope. Kenny has a more obvious self-interest in his view: if people get their feelings out through the theater, it may take attention away from him and his company. People escape into art so they don’t have to reckon with reality.

The question of what art is, what it does, is ultimately deeply personal and subjective.

None of the three gets the last word, and I don’t think the film wants to provide any answer to what is “right” either. I therefore understand that for some this is a film that doesn’t make up its mind, that doesn’t dare to say anything. For me, though, it is a film that would rather have us, the audience, hold an opinion. We are to arrive at the answer ourselves.

Layer 3: Satire of the Satire?

For what is satire? What do we use it for, and what do we want it to be? When the whole world is burning because someone threw a match, we make satire of it. Why? When we portray cartoonish, villainous clowns whom we, the audience, can comfortably laugh at and hate, what does that mean? If it has no consequences in the real world, if we don’t do anything about it afterwards, isn’t it then just entertainment? What is the satire once it’s over? Are film and theater finished when the curtain falls or the credits start to roll? Do we just walk out of the auditorium and back into reality?

Maybe. And maybe it doesn’t need to be anything other than entertainment. Maybe art is a form of communication, a way of letting out frustration, joy, love and sadness before we go back to our everyday lives.

Or is it something more? Does art create action? Does it affect society? Do you leave the cinema a little more willing to protest against climate change?

How highly should we hold art and culture?

I think Iñárritu wanted us to think about all these questions. To reflect on our own relationship to art: what standards we set, and what we expect of it.

Layer 4: What Do We Do Afterwards?

The last thing that happens in the film is that a “level four” warning plays through the speakers in the theater hall. The audience puts on their gas masks and walks out into a flooded, dystopian world. The camera follows them, we see military personnel and drones flying through the air, and finally it rests on a military vehicle while the credits roll. Then the “level four” warning sounds for us in the cinema too.

I interpret it as Iñárritu asking: are you going to let this happen to us too, while you watch my film? For aren’t we in the cinema exactly the same as the audience in the dystopian theater, the ones who laugh at and hate villains while the world heads toward catastrophe? It’s exaggerated, of course, we don’t necessarily end up in that future, but the question still stands: what is the difference between us and them? This differs from Digger’s claim that we are all guilty. The ending poses it as a question, not to lay blame on anyone, but perhaps to say: for the people in the film this is their reality, we still have the chance to do something.

I also think Iñárritu points to the gap between progressive art and the political zeitgeist we are slowly moving into. Nature and climate have never been more important in art and culture, and yet right-wing parties that don’t believe in man-made climate change are among the largest in many countries. What does it matter that we are progressive and enjoy progressive art, when we seemingly fail to use it for political action? I don’t want to conclude that the progressive audience is passive, there are many who are in fact politically active, but to me there is a hint of political self-satisfaction in being so. We pat each other on the back when we cheer that the villain loses, and then we put on the masks and walk out of the auditorium. The question the film asks us then becomes: what do we choose to do afterwards? That of course also applies to this text I am writing myself.

A small comment on the critics: I find it funny that a film that questions our expectations of art has received such mixed reviews from critics, precisely those who hold art in the highest regard. Much of the criticism is of course quite concrete, and I don’t think it’s a perfect film. Some of the weaker aspects are that the daughter storyline isn’t used much and some of the conversations can be a bit too explanatory. Just a small, quirky observation.
