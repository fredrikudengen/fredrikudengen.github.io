The Brutalist is an incredibly grand and complex epic. I got a bit nervous when I first saw the running time, but all the points and themes it takes up, and not least the story it tries to convey, justify the length. It’s rare to experience films as complex as this. The Godfather, Yi Yi and There Will Be Blood are some examples. Capitalism, Nazism, racism, persecution, drugs, love and hate are just some of the themes it covers. A film that uses architecture to tell something much bigger than itself.

László Tóth: Is there a better description of a cube than that of its construction?

At first I didn’t understand this line, but now I do. László tries to let things be “as they are”, and that is an overarching plot point throughout the film. He is constantly reminded that he isn’t allowed to be as he is, because he is different. We see this very clearly through his cousin Attila, who is an image of how you have to fit in to achieve The American Dream™. Attila has assimilated into the American world. He has become Catholic and anglicized his surname. László doesn’t want this, he wants to be himself. When Erzsébet, his wife, introduces herself at the dinner, she says they can call her Elizabeth if it’s easier, which László takes badly. He has just fled ethnic persecution in Europe, and now he recognizes it again. Corbet, the director, himself describes The Brutalist as the story of “a person who flees fascism only to meet capitalism”.

We meet capitalism through Van Buren. He is the very image of American capitalism and abundance. He wants to appear cultivated himself, or at least be seen as someone who enjoys high culture. He often talks with László and finds their conversations “intellectually stimulating”. We sense that he wants to use László’s new and groundbreaking style to promote something he himself is not. László’s brutalism focuses on equality, which we see in his reaction to the building being given a Christian room: “It’s a gathering place for all kinds of people”. I especially remember the scenes where László is first invited to their home. We get very stylistic close-ups of people gambling with money and jewelry, while László talks about his past in the camps. Class divisions and racial discrimination are two of the film’s main points and touch on a lot around intersectionality. The whole struggle between László and Van Buren culminates in László being raped, both metaphorically and literally, by the man who hired him. Their relationship strongly reminded me of Parasite (2019). Van Buren sees László as the parasite, the representative of the underclass. He talks about “handouts”, and in the rape scene itself he calls him outright “a societal leech”. But as in Parasite, the whole point is that the upper class are really the true parasites. After all, it is Van Buren who came to László for help with his building project, and then he makes himself a god to him. This is an image of how the upper class treats Jews, minorities and artists.

László’s style, brutalism, is perhaps the most controversial architectural style there is. It at least challenged traditional views of architecture, and it was meant to promote equality and rebuilding after the war. At the same time it works as a reminder of the past, because it is an incredible consequence of the time it was created in, the 1950s. As is explained in the film, it is a testament to what life was like in the camps: high walls, concrete, imprisoning. I really liked the description “A way of directing their inhabitant’s perception to the world as it is. The inherent laws of concrete things such as mountains and rock define them. They indicate nothing. They tell nothing. They simply are.”

A question that remains, then, is why we should carry these memories forward. If this period was so traumatic for László, why does he want to remember it? Well, perhaps that is exactly why. He wants to remember it, or rather: the world should remember it. That is why I think people who call brutalism ugly are really missing the point. Of course you can criticize brutalist buildings, because they are often built in the wrong place, among styles they don’t go with (Realfagsbygget). But saying they are ugly is a bit superficial, and it also expresses a very functional view of architecture. Should art only look nice, or can it reflect something more? Either way, I now have a new respect and understanding for brutalism.

I liked the scenes with Orazio, the Italian. He tells them: “No one knows the quarries like us. We led members of the Esercito Nazionale Repubblicano into the caves here, captured them, dropped stones on them.” The whole scene gives a feeling that for them it isn’t just stone we’re talking about, but almost a friend, one who has fought alongside them through the war. I really liked the image of Van Buren putting his head against the stone, so that we hear a heartbeat. Or when Orazio “waters” the stone first, as if it were a living being with a soul.

The characters were all fantastic. I think several could have deserved an Oscar for their acting, but Brody’s was well deserved. Still, I think Guy Pearce as Van Buren steals the show every time he’s on screen. He is so incredibly captivating, and also quite an interesting character. Joe Alwyn as the son is also very good. Felicity Jones, as the wife, is damn good. I love her character and the whole plot around her and László. I would say she was the hero of the film. She stood by her husband through everything they had been through. I especially liked when László said “I will figure it out” and she replies “WE will figure it out”. The scene where he chooses to give her the heroin is also incredibly raw. The same goes for when she starts working in New York to support them, even though she would much rather be with him as much as possible. It shows how much they value each other. I also liked Isaach de Bankolé as Gordon, but would have liked to see more of him and his son. There was a cool parallel between him and László that I feel could have been explored more.

Two of the most important people are a Jewish man and a Black man, yet racism isn’t talked about openly. The racism and antisemitism in the film are shown in a more underlying and creeping way. There are small hints everywhere: “we brought her here for some peace and respite only to discover a strange Negro man roaming around our property”. We, like László in the car, realize that they don’t want them there. Like Attila’s wife, they want to throw them out, because they don’t fit the ideal of the American nuclear family, the Nuclear Family™.

Religion is also important in the film. László attends Jewish services because he wants to hold on to himself. The institute itself has a big cross, after all. Even though László wasn’t Christian, he perhaps sees the work as a comfort, as a way of coming closer to God. And then we come to the end of Van Buren’s story. What actually happened there? He disappeared, and then we were led to the chapel with the cross glowing on the stone. It is a metaphor, but for what? Forgiveness? Sin? Does his disappearance represent the traumas, like Nazism, disappearing for László?

The film also briefly touches on Israel. I thought that was brave. As everyone probably knows, Israel has a really terrible history, but the film in no way tries to humanize the founding of the country. Erzsébet brings this up in the film: “Does it somehow make us less Jewish that we are here?” (in America). The film shows how important Israel is to Jews, without really saying anything more than that. Antisemitism is so integrated in society that I understand why they left. The production and the sets were also absolutely fantastic. I would say they rival There Will Be Blood.

I think I understand the ending now. It’s actually an incredibly funny ending, which stands in contrast to the rest of the music: EDM, Venice, a nice little speech with a good saying to finish. It’s everything the superficial West likes. It is almost comical that László wins a prize for his architecture now that we have learned the backstory and what the works meant to him. It’s as if he is receiving a prize for the suffering he has been through. I also think the saying is both representative of the film and works as satire: “no matter what the others try and sell you, it is the destination, not the journey.” Boiling down an entire film, or an architectural style, to one sentence becomes comical. But at the same time I think it suits the film. Now that you know the whole journey of this institute, you perhaps have more understanding of it (even if you think it’s ugly)? Perhaps it isn’t the finished buildings, the completed works, that are important, but the lives they touch?

All in all, I thought the film was intellectually stimulating.
